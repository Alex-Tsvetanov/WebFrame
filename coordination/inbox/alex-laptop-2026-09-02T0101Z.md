## The four cells

Two rates, not one, and that is a change from the brief I want to flag. Re-reading
"the churn cells at a rate low enough that neither is throughput-limited", I took
"neither" as neither backend rather than neither shape, because the comparison is
backend against backend within a shape. Holding one rate across both shapes forced a
choice between running keep-alive far below what it can do and running churn above what
it can do, and the second is the confound being avoided.

A first attempt did offer 2000 to both. Keep-alive met it exactly; churn delivered 84.6%
and ran three seconds behind its own schedule. The probe's 10500 establishments a second
was over LOOPBACK; over the veth pair an establishment pays a driver and a receive
softirq that loopback skips, which is why the project's own network churn table runs 50
to 800. So keep-alive offers 10000 and churn offers 400.

Every cell met its offered rate, which is the condition that was missing last time:

| cell                 | backend  | offered | achieved | syscalls/req | accepted |
|----------------------|----------|---------|----------|--------------|----------|
| keep-alive           | epoll    | 10000   | 9999.79  | 7.1338       | no       |
| keep-alive           | io_uring | 10000   | 9999.77  | 25.6528      | **yes**  |
| churn                | epoll    | 400     | 399.99   | 30.4178      | no       |
| churn                | io_uring | 400     | 399.99   | 586.4987     | no       |

Three of the four are still refused for frequency drift, now 2.5%, 3.8% and 4.2% against
the 2% limit, down from 26.5% at the 2000 rate. Drift falls as the machine gets busier,
which is consistent with a mostly idle 16-core laptop having a noisy mean clock rather
than with throttling. The one cell that passed is the busiest.

## The finding: io_uring_enter is time-proportional, not request-proportional

| cell                 | event            | count     | per request | per second |
|----------------------|------------------|-----------|-------------|------------|
| epoll    keep-alive  | epoll_wait       |   281874  |    1.127    |   11275    |
| epoll    churn       | epoll_wait       |    30869  |    3.087    |    1235    |
| io_uring keep-alive  | io_uring_enter   |  5903064  |   23.612    |  236123    |
| io_uring churn       | io_uring_enter   |  5688668  |  568.867    |  227547    |

The request rate differs by 25x between those two io_uring cells. The enter rate differs
by **1.04x**. Over the same 25x change, epoll_wait moves 9.13x.

So the io_uring backend issues io_uring_enter at a roughly constant ~230000 a second
whatever the load, across four workers, which is about 58000 per worker per second. The
feasibility probe is consistent with this and I misread it at the time: 950312 enters
over a 13 second window with one worker is about 73000 a second, which I reported as
"4.752 per request" because the load happened to be 20000 requests a second. It was
never per request.

That is a busy-poll, not a per-request cost. Dividing it by requests produces a number
that says more about how many requests happened to arrive during a fixed amount of
polling than about what a request costs. "586 syscalls per request" is really "the poll
loop ran for twenty seconds and only ten thousand requests arrived to divide it by".

### What this means for the two papers

Do not put 3.596x or 19.281x in the mechanism section as a per-request comparison. They
are arithmetically correct and they measure the poll loop.

What can be said, and I think is worth saying, is stronger and more specific:

  - epoll's syscall count tracks work. Its per-request figures are stable and
    interpretable: 1 recvfrom, 1 sendto, 2 epoll_ctl and 2 clock_gettime per request
    keep-alive; plus 1 accept4 and 1 close per request under churn.
  - io_uring's does not track work at all on this implementation. Its cost is a function
    of wall time and worker count.
  - Therefore the completion model as implemented here is not paying a per-request
    syscall price; it is paying a per-second one, and any comparison of the two models
    on syscalls has to normalise by time, or by time and worker count, rather than by
    requests.

That is a finding about this implementation, as you said of the earlier 4.75 number, and
it belongs in the paper as such. It also suggests a concrete thing to look at in the
backend: whether the worker loop is calling io_uring_enter with a short timeout in a
spin rather than blocking, since 58000 a second per worker with 400 requests a second
arriving is a loop that is almost entirely empty.

I have not touched the backend. Measuring it before tuning it was your instruction on
the earlier number and it applies more here.

### The epoll breakdown, which is interpretable

    epoll keep-alive, per request:  recvfrom 1.000, sendto 1.000, epoll_ctl 2.001,
                                    clock_gettime 2.002, epoll_wait 1.127,
                                    accept4 0.000, close 0.000
    epoll churn, per request:       accept4 1.006, close 1.006, recvfrom 2.007,
                                    sendto 1.000, epoll_ctl 6.026, clock_gettime 9.293,
                                    epoll_wait 3.087

Churn costs epoll 4.264x keep-alive per request, and the composition says where it goes:
an accept and a close appear, recvfrom doubles, epoll_ctl triples and clock_gettime goes
from 2 to 9.3. That last one is worth someone's attention on its own: nine clock reads
per established connection is a lot, and unlike the io_uring number it does scale with
work.
